When we only work on these type of problems by hand, it constrains the size and nature of the problem. These problems tend to have integer coefficients, they tend to have nice solutions, they take few steps to solve, they are abstract in the worst sense of that word: devoid of any context because then it wouldn't be "math" To be clear, I am not arguing that this is how math is done, nor that we should not teach math "by hand". If you've ever taken my Algebra 2 or PreCalculus courses, then you've learned to solve systems of linear equations with Octave. My students complete those problems twice, once by hand and then a second time with Octave. Two important aspects of having students do this are: 1 - While they already completed the work by hand, they now know that computers can do it. 2 - If they want to work on bigger problems that involve this mathematics, the computation is done for them. This opens up an entire world of new problems they could not do without such a tool.

Broken Stick Problem

Given a stick broken at two random places, what is the probability that the three pieces can form a triangle?
This is a classic probability problem. No spoilers please! Think about it for a minute. What do we, or especially a student, do when we are faced with a problem we don't know the theory behind? We play with it. We feel it out. We start to explore it numerically.

Break a Stick

  1. Generate two random numbers, Break1 and Break2
  2. Determine the Left break and Right break:
    • Left = min(Break1, Break2)
    • Right = max(Break1, Break2)
  3. The three lengths are:
    • L1 = Left
    • L2 = Right - Left
    • L3 = 1 - Right
Let's say the stick has length 1, and we pick two random locations, break1 and break2.

Observations become Conjectures

  • The longest that the shortest piece can be is 1/3
  • The shortest that the longest piece can be is 1/3
  • The shortest that the middle piece can be is around 0
  • The longest that the middle piece can be is around 1/2
  • The longest piece has to be shorter than 1/2 for a triangle
  • The longest piece is shorter than 1/2 around 1/4 of the time
These are all observations, not proofs, but they are observations that can be proved. Experimental mathematics leads to discoveries and conjectures which can subsequently be proved. This is the connection between empirical experimentation with theory.
