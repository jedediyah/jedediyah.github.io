We assume that there is a relationship between drop height and bounce height. A real physical relationship, that is likely very complicated. But we will choose a simple model that we hope will *approximate* the true relationship.

How high does a bouncy ball bounce?

\[ \hat{Y} = \hat{\beta} X \]

\(\hat{\beta}\) is a model parameter: a tuneable value in our model
In fact, for our bouncy ball data, our model is extremely simple. It has one parameter, which is the slope of the line. You may have heard people talking about AI models with billions of parameters. Those models require much more data to train because there is so much tuning to do for that many parameters.

The residual error between the observed data \((x_i, y_i)\) and the model is:
\[ e_{i} = y_i - \hat{y}_i \]
where \[ \hat{y}_i = \hat{\beta} x_{i} \]

The residual error between the observed data \((x_i, y_i)\) and the model is:
\[ e_{i} = y_i - \hat{y}_i \]
The sum of squared error is the sum of squares $e_i^2$
\[ SSE = \sum_{i=1}^{n} e_{i}^{2}\]

 
We do a parameter sweep, looking for a value of beta that minimizes the Sum of Squared Error (SSE).

Model Interpretation

What does the parameter in our model mean?
\( \hat{h}_{bounce} = \hat{\beta} h_{drop} \)

Model Interpretation

\( \hat{h}_{bounce} = \hat{\beta} h_{drop} \)
\(\hat{\beta}\) is the slope, \(\frac{h_{bounce}}{h_{drop}}\)
This is the ratio of bounce height to drop height
and means we predict \(h_{bounce}\) to be 64% of \(h_{drop}\)
